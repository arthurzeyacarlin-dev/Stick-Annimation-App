## Arthur's story (path C3–C7)

**1 · live day 7 · Subject: I was 5 and I closed my eyes · Preview: Where Diamond Animator really started.**
Can I tell you where Diamond Animator really started?
I was five. I had just learned how to use the TV remote, and I found a show where Lego characters fought each other. I loved it.
So I did what felt natural. I closed my eyes, put my hands up and started making up whole fights in my head. I never stopped. I still do it almost every day.
My parents didn’t get it. They weren’t mean about it. They just couldn’t see what I could see.
**Try this today:** close your eyes for 30 seconds and play one short scene in your head. Who is in it? What is the very first thing that moves? Write that down in one sentence. That sentence is the start of an animation.
Next time I’ll tell you about the day I found a way to show people what was in my head, and why it almost made me quit.

**2 · live day 9 · Subject: Weeks of work, and I didn't even like it · Preview: The wall every new animator hits.**
Last time I told you about the fights I made up in my head. Then I found animation apps, and I thought: finally, I can show my parents.
I really tried. FlipaClip for almost half a year. Stick Nodes for about half a year. Adobe Animate, which I still use. Drawing books for almost a year.
Every time it took weeks of work. And after all of that, I often didn’t even like what I made.
Here is what I learned from it, and you can use it right now: **make it short.** A beginner’s first animation should be 3 seconds, not 3 minutes. One move. A jump. A wave. A punch.
A finished 3-second animation teaches you more than a 3-minute one you give up on.

**2 · live day 17 · Subject: The one thing that makes animation look real · Preview: It isn't drawing.**
Most people think good animation is good drawing. It isn’t. It’s timing.
Two rules that work for every move:
- **Slow in, slow out.** Things start slowly, speed up, then slow down before they stop. Nothing real starts at full speed.
- **Heavy things are slow. Light things are fast.** A giant takes a long time to lift his foot. A mouse doesn’t.
Next time you watch a cartoon, count how long a character holds still before a big move. That pause is doing most of the work.
These are 2 of the 12 animation principles. The other 10 are on the free cheat sheet:
[Open the cheat sheet → https://diamondanimator.com/free-guide/?got=1]

**3 · live day 19 · Subject: 5 animation ideas when you're stuck · Preview: Steal any of these.**
The hardest part of animating is the blank page. So here are 5 ideas you can take:
- A stick figure tries to sit on a chair that keeps moving.
- Someone opens a box, and what’s inside scares them.
- A robot learns to dance, badly, then well.
- A chase that ends because the one in front stops to tie a shoe.
- Someone throws a ball up and it never comes down.
Notice what they share: **a small surprise.** You don’t need a big story. You need one thing the viewer didn’t expect.
Make up a sixth one and reply with it. I’d love to read it.

**4 · live day 23 · Subject: Why stiff animations look stiff · Preview: Nothing in nature moves in a straight line.**
If your animation ever looked stiff or robotic, this is almost always why.
**Living things move in curves.** Swing your arm: your hand draws a curve, not a line. A thrown ball flies in a curve. A head turning dips a little in the middle.
Animators call these arcs. Check any move you make: if you connected the hand from frame to frame, would it draw a smooth curve? If it zig-zags, that’s the stiffness.
The second fix: don’t move everything at once. The body goes first, the arms follow, the hair or the cape comes last.
Two small ideas, and they fix most stiff animation.

## Keep in touch tips (path G1–G8)

**1 · live day 35 · Subject: The bouncing ball secret · Preview: The first thing every animator learns.**
Every animation school starts with the same exercise: a bouncing ball. Here’s the secret inside it.
**Squash and stretch.** When the ball hits the ground, it squashes flat. When it flies up, it stretches long. It keeps the same amount of “stuff”, it just changes shape.
It works on everything. A character landing from a jump squashes down, then stretches up. A face stretches in surprise.
The more you squash and stretch, the more cartoony it feels. The less, the more real. You choose.
Try it on one landing this week.

**2 · live day 42 · Subject: Could you tell what he's doing from his shadow? · Preview: The silhouette test.**
Here’s a test animators use on every important pose.
**Fill the character in solid black.** Can you still tell what he’s doing? If yes, it’s a strong pose. If it’s a blob, it isn’t.
The usual fix: move the arms and legs away from the body. A hand in front of the chest disappears. A hand out to the side reads at once.
Stick figures are great for learning this, because a stick figure is almost a silhouette already.
Look at your favourite animation and pause on a big moment. I bet it passes the test.

**3 · live day 49 · Subject: Do nothing. On purpose. · Preview: Why the best moments don't move.**
New animators keep everything moving all the time. It feels like that’s the job.
But the moments people remember are often the ones where **nothing moves.**
The character sees something. He freezes. One beat. Two beats. Then he runs.
That freeze is called a hold. It gives the viewer time to understand, and it makes the next move feel twice as fast.
Rule of thumb: before any big move, and after any big surprise, hold still for about half a second.

**4 · live day 56 · Subject: Make it too much. Then it's just right. · Preview: Why real-looking moves look weak.**
If you copy a real jump exactly, it looks small and boring in animation. Weird, but true.
**Animation needs exaggeration.** Bend lower than a real person would. Jump higher. Lean further into a run. Open the mouth wider.
A good way to find the right amount: go way too far on purpose, then pull back a little. You almost always end up further than you thought you should.
Describing a scene works the same way. “He’s surprised” is OK. “He jumps back so hard he falls over” is a cartoon.

**5 · live day 70 · Subject: Animate to a beat · Preview: A trick that makes timing easy.**
Here’s a trick that makes timing much easier: use a beat.
Clap a steady rhythm: one, two, three, four. Now put your moves on the claps. Step, step, jump, land.
Fights work this way: hit, hit, pause, big hit. Jokes work this way too: set up, set up, surprise.
When something feels off in your animation and you can’t say why, clap it out. Usually one move is landing between the beats.

**6 · live day 84 · Subject: The little moves that make it feel alive · Preview: What happens after the main move.**
The main move tells the story. The little moves after it make it feel alive.
- He stops running, and his arms swing forward once more.
- She lands, and her hair comes down a moment later.
- He points, and his other hand makes a small fist.
Animators call this secondary action and follow-through. You only need one per scene.
**Try it:** pick the last frame of any move and ask yourself, what would keep moving for a moment? Add that.

**7 · live day 98 · Subject: Finished beats perfect · Preview: The habit that makes animators good.**
The animators who get good aren’t the ones who make one perfect animation. They’re the ones who finish a lot of small ones.
I learned that the hard way. I used to spend weeks on one animation and then not even like it.
**A challenge:** make five tiny animations, each 3 seconds, each with one idea. Don’t fix them. Just finish them.
By the fifth, you’ll be better than you were on the first. That’s the whole secret.
